## What does vitamin D do?

Vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium from the intestine and maintain adequate calcium and phosphate concentrations required for normal bone mineralization.

It also contributes to:

- Bone growth and remodeling
- Normal muscle function
- Calcium homeostasis
- Maintenance of skeletal health

Insufficient vitamin D can impair bone mineralization and, when severe, contribute to **rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults**.

Vitamin D therefore works closely with calcium and other factors involved in skeletal health.

## How does the body obtain vitamin D?

There are three major sources:

**Sunlight → Food → Supplements**

### Sunlight

When ultraviolet-B (UVB) radiation reaches uncovered skin, it initiates vitamin D synthesis.

The vitamin D produced in the skin is subsequently converted through metabolic steps in the liver and kidneys into biologically active forms.

Vitamin D production can be affected by:

- Season
- Time of day
- Geographic location
- Cloud cover
- Air pollution
- Skin pigmentation
- Age
- Clothing
- Sunscreen
- Amount of skin exposed

UVB does not pass effectively through ordinary glass, so sunlight coming through a window is not equivalent to direct outdoor exposure for vitamin D synthesis.

### Do not deliberately seek sunburn

Sun exposure has to be balanced with skin-cancer prevention.

The goal should not be to obtain vitamin D by intentionally exposing the skin to excessive UV radiation or allowing the skin to burn.

## Food sources of vitamin D

Vitamin D occurs naturally in relatively few foods.

Useful sources include:

- Fatty fish such as salmon, sardines, trout, herring and mackerel
- Egg yolks
- Some meats
- Liver
- Fortified foods
- Vitamin D-containing supplements

The availability of fortified foods varies between countries and products.

For many people, obtaining all required vitamin D from naturally occurring food sources alone can be difficult.

## Who is more likely to have low vitamin D?

Vitamin D inadequacy is more likely in people with limited vitamin D production or absorption.

Examples include people who:

- Spend very little time outdoors
- Have limited skin exposure to sunlight
- Have darker skin pigmentation
- Are older
- Have conditions affecting fat absorption
- Have undergone bariatric surgery
- Have certain liver or kidney disorders
- Have dietary restrictions that reduce vitamin D intake

Obesity can also be associated with lower circulating vitamin D concentrations.

Having a risk factor does **not automatically prove deficiency**.

## What are the symptoms of vitamin D deficiency?

Mild vitamin D insufficiency may produce no obvious symptoms.

More significant deficiency can affect the skeleton and muscles.

Possible manifestations include:

- Bone pain
- Muscle weakness
- Reduced physical function
- Increased risk of bone problems

Severe deficiency can result in **osteomalacia in adults** and **rickets in children**.

Symptoms such as fatigue or generalized body aches are nonspecific and should not automatically be attributed to vitamin D deficiency.

## How is vitamin D status assessed?

Vitamin D status is generally assessed using a blood measurement of **25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D]**.

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes that:

- **≥20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L)** is adequate for most people for bone and overall health.
- **<12 ng/mL (30 nmol/L)** is too low and may affect health.
- **>50 ng/mL (125 nmol/L)** may be associated with adverse effects.

Interpretation should be considered in clinical context rather than treating a laboratory number in isolation.

Routine testing is also not necessarily required for every healthy individual.

## How much vitamin D do you need?

Requirements vary according to age and physiological circumstances.

For example, the NHS states that adults require **10 micrograms (400 IU) of vitamin D per day** as a general reference, while recommendations and supplementation policies can vary between countries and populations.

One important conversion is:

**1 microgram (mcg) vitamin D = 40 IU**

Therefore:

**10 mcg = 400 IU**

When discussing vitamin D intake, always check whether the dose is being expressed in **mcg or IU**.

## Should everyone take a vitamin D supplement?

Not necessarily.

The need for supplementation depends on factors such as:

- Dietary intake
- Sun exposure
- Risk of deficiency
- Age
- Health conditions
- Pregnancy or lactation
- Medical advice

Some public-health systems recommend routine low-dose supplementation for particular populations or seasons.

A person with confirmed deficiency may require a different treatment regimen from someone simply trying to maintain adequate vitamin D status.

**Treatment doses should not be confused with routine maintenance doses.**

## Why taking very high doses can be dangerous

Vitamin D is fat-soluble, and excessive supplementation can lead to toxicity.

Excess vitamin D can increase calcium absorption excessively and cause **hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria**.

Possible manifestations include:

- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Muscle weakness
- Confusion
- Excessive thirst
- Frequent urination
- Kidney stones

Severe toxicity can cause kidney failure, cardiac arrhythmias and other serious complications.

Excessive vitamin D intake is generally caused by supplements rather than sunlight.

For adults, the NIH-established tolerable upper intake level is **100 mcg (4,000 IU) per day** from all sources, although clinicians may use higher doses temporarily to treat confirmed deficiency when medically indicated.

Therefore:

> **More vitamin D is not automatically better.**

## Vitamin D, calcium and bone health

Vitamin D and calcium work together in maintaining skeletal health.

Vitamin D supports intestinal calcium absorption, while calcium provides an essential mineral component of bone.

A healthy bone-health strategy therefore involves more than vitamin D supplementation.

It can include:

**Adequate nutrition + appropriate physical activity + adequate calcium + vitamin D sufficiency + healthy lifestyle**

Weight-bearing and resistance exercise are particularly relevant because mechanical loading contributes to bone and musculoskeletal health.

## Vitamin D and physical fitness

Vitamin D should not be treated as a general performance-enhancing supplement.

Adequate vitamin D is important for normal physiological function, including skeletal and muscular health, but taking additional vitamin D above adequate levels does not automatically improve athletic performance.

For someone with deficiency, correcting that deficiency may be clinically important.

For someone who already has adequate vitamin D status, indiscriminately increasing supplementation is unlikely to provide the same rationale.

## Can sunlight alone correct vitamin D deficiency?

Not necessarily.

Sunlight can contribute substantially to vitamin D production, but the amount produced varies considerably between individuals and circumstances.

People with limited sun exposure, certain medical conditions, or established deficiency may require dietary or supplemental intervention.

A confirmed deficiency should therefore be managed according to its severity and clinical context rather than relying exclusively on unmeasured sun exposure.

## Common mistakes about vitamin D

### "More sunlight always means more vitamin D."

Not indefinitely. Vitamin D synthesis is regulated, while excessive UV exposure increases skin damage and skin-cancer risk.

### "Everyone needs high-dose vitamin D."

No. High-dose treatment is different from routine supplementation.

### "If I feel tired, I must have vitamin D deficiency."

Fatigue has many possible causes and is not specific to vitamin D deficiency.

### "Vitamin D supplements are completely harmless."

Excessive supplementation can cause toxicity.

### "Vitamin D alone is enough for strong bones."

Bone health depends on multiple factors, including calcium, physical activity, nutrition, age, hormones and other health factors.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I get vitamin D from sunlight through a window?

No. UVB radiation required for skin vitamin D production does not effectively pass through ordinary glass.

### Is vitamin D present in milk?

It depends on the country and product. Some milk products are fortified with vitamin D, while others are not. Always check the nutrition label.

### Is vitamin D deficiency common?

Risk varies substantially between populations. Limited sunlight exposure, darker skin, older age, malabsorption and certain medical conditions can increase risk.

### Should I get a vitamin D test?

Testing is most useful when there is a clinical reason to suspect deficiency or when monitoring treatment is appropriate. Routine testing is not necessarily required for every healthy person.

### Can I take vitamin D every day?

Some people are advised to take daily vitamin D, depending on age, season, risk factors and local recommendations. The appropriate dose matters, and high-dose supplementation should not be started without appropriate medical guidance.

### Can vitamin D improve muscle strength?

Adequate vitamin D is important for normal muscle function. In people with deficiency, correction may be clinically beneficial, but supplementation above adequate levels should not automatically be expected to improve strength or athletic performance.

## Conclusion

Vitamin D is an important nutrient involved in **calcium regulation, bone mineralization and normal muscle function**.

The body's vitamin D status is influenced by:

**Sunlight + diet + supplementation + individual physiology**

A sensible approach is not to chase extremely high vitamin D levels, but to maintain adequate status through appropriate sunlight exposure, dietary sources and supplementation when indicated.

For people with suspected or confirmed deficiency, individualized assessment is preferable to self-prescribing high doses.

From an Ayurvedic perspective, vitamin D can be considered within the broader context of **Ahara, Agni, Dhatu, daily routine and individualized health**, while modern physiology provides the biochemical framework for understanding its specific role.

The objective is simple:

**adequate vitamin D—not excessive vitamin D.**
